# Sourcing Microgreens for NYC Kitchens: Reliability, Yield, and Specs

For restaurant buyers, microgreens are a vendor problem before they’re a plating problem. Missed cuts, wet packs, and mystery mixes blow food cost faster than any garnish upcharge can recover.

This draft is for chefs, sous, and purchasing leads sourcing microgreens and edible flowers for New York kitchens—what to put on the PO, what to check on receiving, and how to judge a supplier beyond a pretty sample box.

## What “good” looks like on receiving

Score every delivery the way you’d score herbs:

- **Temperature and integrity.** Packs cold, lids intact, no crushed product.
- **Dryness.** Excess moisture is the fast path to slime.
- **Trim and maturity.** True micro stage—usable stem and leaf, not leggy or flowering unless specified.
- **Label clarity.** Variety name matches the order guide (not just “mix”).
- **Usable yield.** After you discard unusable tips, do you still get the covers you planned?

If a supplier fails receiving checks twice in a month, the relationship is already costing you line time.

## Specs worth writing into the order guide

Don’t rely on chat threads. Put this on the guide:

1. **Exact SKUs** (e.g. micro basil, sunflower, viola)—not “chef’s mix” unless you truly want surprise.
2. **Pack size** matched to shift volume (oversize packs are a waste machine).
3. **Delivery days** aligned to prep (many NYC programs run midweek + endweek patterns).
4. **Substitution rules** (approve in advance or refuse silent swaps).
5. **Hold expectations** (how the product should perform under your storage).

FuelFoods culinary programs are built around restaurant cadence—professional packs, variety depth, and delivery meant for kitchens rather than retail browsing. Your job is to mirror that with a clean internal spec.

## Food cost: measure covers, not clamshells

Track:

- Covers plated per pack on hero dishes
- Spoilage percentage by SKU
- Labor minutes spent cleaning or re-sorting

A slightly higher unit price with higher usable yield and less sorting often wins. Cheap wet product is expensive.

## Risk and consistency (the quiet buyer criteria)

Ask growers direct questions:

- How soon after harvest do you deliver to Manhattan / boroughs / nearby markets you serve?
- What does a shortage week look like—substitutions, early notice, or silence?
- Can you hold a standing order with seasonal adds?
- Are flowers food-grade and grown for culinary use?
- Any certifications or buyer paperwork your restaurant group needs (including MWBE where relevant)?

FuelFoods positions as an NYC MWBE-certified supplier serving professional kitchens with microgreens and edible flowers—useful when group purchasing or city-adjacent accounts care about that paperwork. Still verify current cert status through your normal vendor onboarding.

## Sample box vs. standing order

A sample box proves flavor and look. A standing order proves operations. Run a two-week pilot on 4–6 SKUs tied to live menu items before you expand the rail. Kill SKUs that don’t earn two uses or one high-volume prep.

## Pair with plating and flower SOPs

Sourcing only works if the line has portion rules and hold SOPs. See the companion drafts on plating microgreens and edible-flower handling so purchasing and the pass tell the same story.

## Bottom line

Buy microgreens like a produce contract: named SKUs, delivery cadence, receiving standards, and yield math. The best garnish program is the one that shows up cold, dry, and identical on Friday.
