## What microgreens are in a professional kitchen

Microgreens are young vegetable, herb, or edible-plant seedlings harvested after the cotyledons have opened and the first true leaves have begun to form. In kitchen language: they are a **cut garnish and flavor finish**, not a salad base and not a living tray you leave under a heat lamp.

What you are buying when you open a restaurant pack:

- **Concentrated flavor** — basil, cilantro, shiso, celery, chives, and sunflower taste like themselves, only tighter and brighter.
- **Plate architecture** — height, color contrast, and a clean edge that photographs well without looking fussy.
- **Predictable portioning** — a pinch or small scatter per plate, so cost per cover stays manageable when waste is controlled.
- **Station flexibility** — cold apps, crudo, composed salads, finished proteins, desserts, and cocktails.

They are harvested young, packed for walk-in storage, and meant to be used as a finishing mise — not cooked down like spinach.

## Microgreens vs. sprouts vs. baby greens

Buying managers get burned when these three get conflated on a purchase order. They are different products with different roles, shelf lives, and handling.

### Sprouts

Sprouts are germinated seeds eaten root, shoot, and seed — often grown in humid environments and harvested before true leaves develop. Classic examples: alfalfa, mung bean, broccoli sprouts. Soft texture; heat and long ticket times collapse them fast. Not interchangeable with micro basil or sunflower on a tasting menu.

### Baby greens / baby leaf

Baby greens are immature salad leaves harvested later than microgreens — baby kale, baby spinach, baby arugula sold by the pound for salads and sauté. They fill volume on the plate. Cost model is ounces per cover, not pinches. Texture and cook behavior differ from micros.

### Microgreens

Microgreens sit between sprouts and baby leaf in plant age, but they are **culinary finishers**: cut above the root, packed as leafy tips and stems, selected for color, aroma, and plate impact. Varieties that matter on NYC menus include micro basil, cilantro, shiso, chives, celery, sunflower, amaranth, bulls blood beet, rainbow mixes — plus edible flowers when the concept calls for them.

| Product | Typical kitchen role | Portion logic |
|--------|----------------------|---------------|
| Sprouts | Sandwich / bowl add-in | Volume scoop |
| Baby greens | Salad base / sauté | Ounces per cover |
| Microgreens | Garnish / aroma finish | Pinch / tweezer |

If your PO says "greens" without specifying **cut microgreens**, you will get the wrong product and the wrong plate.

## Why chefs specify microgreens

Chefs add a SKU because it solves problems on the line.

**Finish without bulk.** A tasting menu or polished appetizer cannot afford a handful of bulk leaf that wilts under a heat lamp. Micros give aroma and color in grams, which protects food cost.

**Consistency across covers.** When the same dish runs 80 covers on a Saturday, the garnish has to look the same at 6:00 and at 10:30. Uniform stem length, clean cut, and no yellowing are easier to standardize than torn herb leaves from a tired bunch.

**Flavor that reads as intentional.** Micro cilantro on ceviche, micro shiso on crudo, micro basil on tomato-burrata: guests taste herb without chewing through a full leaf.

**Cross-station utility.** One SKU list serves garde manger, hot line (as a post-heat finish), pastry, and bar. That matters for hotel F&B and multi-outlet groups consolidating vendors.

**Local story without a lecture.** NYC diners notice "locally grown" when it is true and the plate looks like it. Pair honest sourcing notes with the dish — not miracle claims — and it strengthens the menu card and the server pitch.

**Procurement and MWBE goals.** Many hotel groups, corporate dining programs, and public-facing F&B contracts track MWBE spend. A certified MWBE microgreens supplier helps when vendor scorecards ask for diversity metrics.

## What "restaurant-grade" actually means

Not every clamshell belongs on a professional line. Kitchen-grade product tends to share:

- Clean cut and intact cotyledons — no crushed slurry at the bottom of the pack.
- Correct variety labeling so Expo and garde manger are not guessing.
- Cold-chain habit — harvested and moved into cold storage quickly.
- Usable shelf life — Fuel Foods Culinary notes **7–10 days when properly stored**, which is the window kitchens plan waste around.
- Variety depth — enough SKUs year-round, plus edible flowers for pastry and bar.
- Custom growing when menus demand a specific look for a season or hotel launch.

## Speccing micros on the menu and in purchasing

**Guest-facing:** prefer sensory language — "micro basil," "shiso," "sunflower crunch," "edible viola" — over vague "superfood greens."

**On the PO:** variety name, cut micros (not sprouts), pack size, delivery cadence, and intended station. Train the line: **finish cold or post-heat; do not sweat micros in a hot pan** unless the dish is designed for it. Station technique detail: [plating microgreens for chefs](/blog/plating-microgreens-for-chefs).

**On cost cards:** track grams or pinches per plate, not "handful." Most waste comes from open packs left warm on the pass.

**Edible flowers on the same vendor:** pastry and bar often need blooms. ## Common buying mistakes

1. Ordering sprouts when the chef wanted micros.
2. Treating micros like bulk salad and over-portioning.
3. Staging on a warm pass for an entire service — pull from cold mise in small batches.
4. One SKU for every dish — rainbow mix is useful; it is not a substitute for micro cilantro or micro shiso.
5. Ignoring edible flowers when pastry and bar need blooms from the same vendor.

## FAQ

### Are microgreens the same as sprouts?

No. Sprouts are typically eaten as the whole germinated seed. Microgreens are cut young leafy seedlings used as a culinary finish. Spec "cut microgreens" on POs so receiving does not substitute.

### How long do restaurant microgreens last in a walk-in?

With proper cold storage, quality culinary packs are commonly planned for several days of service. Fuel Foods Culinary states microgreens stay fresh **7–10 days when properly stored**. FIFO the packs, keep them cold, and stage only what the next tickets need.
